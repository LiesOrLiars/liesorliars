Case File: 012 Unsolved

The Axeman of New Orleans

◆Part 1: The City

New Orleans in 1918 was a city that had already survived yellow fever, the Civil War, and Reconstruction. It had survived floods and fires and the particular violence of being a port town where money and desperation met at the waterline every day.

It was not prepared for what started in the spring of 1918.

The French Quarter was a neighbourhood of narrow cobblestone streets, wrought iron balconies, and houses pressed close together in the Louisiana heat. Neighbours knew each other. Doors were sometimes left unlocked. It was a community built on proximity and the assumption that proximity meant safety.

That assumption would not survive the year.

What started arriving in the newspapers in May 1918 was something the city had no category for. Not a robbery. Not a fight between men who knew each other. Something systematic. Something that moved through the night and left its mark on families who had done nothing to invite it.

◆Part 2: The First Attacks

The attack on Joseph and Catherine Maggio on May 23, 1918 was the first that investigators connected to what would later become known as the Axeman case.

Joseph Maggio was an Italian-American grocer who lived above his shop on Upperline Street with his wife Catherine. Their neighbour Andrew Maggio, Joseph's brother, woke in the early hours to the sound of groaning. He broke into the bedroom and found both Joseph and Catherine badly wounded, their throats cut. Catherine died at the scene. Joseph died shortly after.

The method was already distinctive. A panel had been chiselled from the rear door. The weapon used — an axe found at the scene — belonged to Joseph Maggio himself. The killer had not brought his own tool. He had used what was available.

On the floor nearby, written in chalk on the pavement, investigators found the words: Mrs. Maggio is going to sit up tonight just like Mrs. Toney. Nobody was ever able to explain what that meant. Mrs. Toney was never identified.

The police had their first case. They did not yet know they had their first case in a series.

◆Part 3: The Pattern Emerges

Louis Besumer and his companion Harriet Lowe were attacked on June 27, 1918. A panel had been removed from a rear door. Besumer's own axe had been used. Both survived the initial attack though Harriet Lowe died two months later from her injuries.

On August 5, 1918, a pregnant woman named Mrs. Edward Schneider was found beaten in her bed, her husband arriving home to discover her. She survived. She gave birth to a healthy child eight days later.

On August 10, a barber named Joseph Romano was attacked. His nieces Pauline and Mary Bruno heard the sound of a struggle and found their uncle stumbling from his bedroom, his skull fractured. He died two days later.

The pattern was impossible to ignore by August 1918. The victims were predominantly Italian-American. The entry method was always the same — a panel chiselled from a rear door. The axe found at every scene belonged to the victim. The Axeman had never brought his own.

The New Orleans Police Department was investigating. They had no suspects. The attacks continued.

◆Part 4: The Silence

Between August 1918 and March 1919 there were no confirmed attacks. The city breathed.

Detectives worked the open cases. They interviewed neighbours, canvassed the Italian-American community, tracked leads that went nowhere. The investigation was hampered by the fact that New Orleans in 1918 had no centralised crime database, no forensic laboratory, and a police department that was structured more for dealing with civil disorder than systematic crime.

They also had the problem of a community that did not entirely trust them. The Italian-American families of the French Quarter had come to America to build something. They had built grocery shops and barbershops and small businesses. They kept their own counsel where they could.

Some investigators at the time floated the theory that the attacks were connected to the Mafia — that the Axeman was an enforcer settling accounts within the Italian community. That theory was never proven and most historians have since rejected it. The victim selection does not support a targeted criminal enterprise. It supports something more random and therefore more frightening.

Then the letter arrived.

◆Part 5: The Letter

On March 13, 1919, a letter was delivered to the offices of the Times-Picayune newspaper. It was published the same day.

The letter opened by claiming that the writer was not a human being but a fell demon from the hottest hell. It described the writer as being in New Orleans and making a proposition to the city. It stated that on Tuesday night, March 19, 1919, at 12:15 in the morning precisely, the writer would pass over New Orleans. And those who were not at home playing jazz or listening to jazz would get the axe.

The letter warned that jazz-lovers need not fear. It described jazz as a form of music divinely created and stated that those within earshot of it at the appointed hour would be spared.

At the end the letter was signed: The Axeman.

The New Orleans Police Department treated the letter seriously. The city responded accordingly.

On the night of March 19, 1919, every jazz club in New Orleans was packed. Music poured out of windows across the city. Families who had no gramophone borrowed neighbours who did. Sheet music publishers rushed out a composition written specifically for the occasion.

Nobody died that night.

The Axeman had kept his word.

◆Part 6: The Music Night

The response to the letter was not simply compliance. It was something closer to defiance.

New Orleans in 1919 was the birthplace of jazz. The music had been developing in the city for years — born out of the intersection of African American musical traditions, European harmonics, and the specific cultural geography of a port city where every kind of person and every kind of influence eventually arrived.

When the Axeman offered jazz as the price of survival, the city did not merely comply. It celebrated. Bands played in houses that had never hosted music before. Gramophones ran all night. The streets of the French Quarter were not quiet that evening — they were louder than they had ever been.

A song called The Mysterious Axeman's Jazz was composed and performed. The composer was listed only as Joseph John Davilla. The sheet music sold across the city in the days before March 19.

When dawn came and the city was intact, the relief was enormous. But the question nobody could answer remained. Who had written the letter? Who had made the promise? And had they been in New Orleans that night, listening?

◆Part 7: The Final Attacks

The Axeman attacks resumed after the music night.

On March 10, 1919, before the letter had been sent, Charles Cortimiglia and his wife Rosie were attacked. Their two-year-old daughter Mary was killed. Both parents survived, though Rosie Cortimiglia was severely injured.

The Cortimiglia case became one of the most damaging episodes of the entire investigation. Rosie, in her hospital bed, named their neighbours Iorlando and Frank Jordano as the perpetrators. She identified them specifically and with certainty. Both Jordanos were arrested and tried. Frank Jordano was sentenced to hang. His father Iorlando was sentenced to life imprisonment.

Neither man had committed the attack. Rosie Cortimiglia recanted her testimony in December 1920. She stated publicly that she had lied, that the Jordanos were innocent, and that she had accused them out of long-standing personal enmity with the family. Both men were released.

The Axeman had not been caught. Two innocent men had nearly been executed. The real perpetrator had continued without interruption.

The final confirmed Axeman attack occurred on October 27, 1919, when Mike Pepitone was murdered in his bed while his wife and six children slept in the next room. His wife heard nothing.

◆Part 8: Esther Albano and the Question of the End

On December 2, 1920, a woman named Esther Albano shot and killed a man named Joseph Mumfre on a street in Los Angeles.

Mumfre was a career criminal with a long record in New Orleans. He had served multiple prison terms. When investigators cross-referenced his periods of incarceration with the Axeman attack timeline, they found a striking correlation. Mumfre had been in prison during the periods when there were no Axeman attacks in New Orleans. He had been free during the periods when the attacks occurred.

Esther Albano was the widow of Mike Pepitone, the last confirmed Axeman victim. She told police she had tracked Mumfre to Los Angeles and shot him because she believed he had murdered her husband.

She was tried, convicted of murder, and sentenced to ten years. She served three before being released.

She never provided any evidence connecting Mumfre definitively to the Axeman attacks beyond her own belief. The New Orleans Police Department did not formally close the case on the basis of Mumfre's death. No conviction was ever entered. No confession was ever recorded.

Joseph Mumfre died in the street. The case remained open.

◆Part 9: The Suspect

Joseph Mumfre remains the only person seriously advanced as a suspect in the Axeman case. The circumstantial case against him is suggestive but not conclusive.

His criminal record placed him in New Orleans during the relevant periods. His prison terms correlated with the gaps in the attack timeline. He was known to the Italian-American community of New Orleans from his criminal activities. He had the background and the contacts that could have given him knowledge of which households to target.

But the circumstantial case has significant problems. The correlation between his imprisonments and the attack gaps, while striking, is imperfect. Several periods of Mumfre's freedom do not correspond to Axeman attacks. The attack pattern itself does not obviously suggest the work of an organised criminal pursuing a specific agenda.

None of the surviving victims identified Mumfre or described anyone who clearly matched his description. No physical evidence connected him to any of the crime scenes. No witness placed him near any of the attacks.

Esther Albano believed he was the man who killed her husband. Whether she was right died with Joseph Mumfre on a Los Angeles street in December 1920.

◆Part 10: The Letter Examined

The Axeman letter published in the Times-Picayune on March 13, 1919 remains one of the most analysed documents in American true crime history.

Handwriting experts have examined it. Linguists have studied its register and vocabulary. Historians have placed it in the context of New Orleans culture in 1918 and 1919.

The conclusions are varied. Some analysts believe the letter was written by the Axeman himself — that it represents genuine communication from a killer who wanted to engage with the city and enjoyed the power of doing so. The operational specificity of the letter — the exact time, the exact night, the exact condition — suggests someone confident enough in their own capacity to make and keep a promise.

Other analysts believe the letter was a hoax — written by someone unconnected to the attacks who exploited the city's fear for their own amusement or gratification. Under this reading, the fact that nobody died on March 19 means nothing. Nobody died because the real Axeman had no idea the letter existed.

A third reading suggests the letter was written by someone connected to the attacks but not the primary perpetrator — an associate, a person with knowledge, someone operating at the edge of the case.

None of these readings can be proven. The letter exists. The handwriting was never matched to a known sample. The author was never identified.

◆Part 11: The Victims Named

The confirmed victims in the Axeman case, those whose deaths or serious injuries were formally investigated and connected to the series, are as follows.

Joseph Maggio, murdered May 23, 1918. Catherine Maggio, murdered May 23, 1918. Harriet Lowe, attacked June 27, 1918, died August 1918 from her injuries. Joseph Romano, attacked August 10, 1918, died August 13, 1918. Mary Cortimiglia, two years old, murdered March 10, 1919. Mike Pepitone, murdered October 27, 1919.

Louis Besumer, Mrs. Edward Schneider, Charles Cortimiglia, and Rosie Cortimiglia survived their attacks.

The question of how many victims the Axeman actually claimed is complicated by the fact that investigators at the time were not certain which attacks to include in the series and which to treat as separate crimes. Some researchers have identified additional attacks that may belong to the same perpetrator. Others argue the confirmed list should be shorter.

What is not disputed is that at least six people died and at least four more were seriously injured across a sixteen-month period in New Orleans. The perpetrator was never identified. No arrest was ever made. No trial was ever held.

◆Part 12: Why It Matters

The Axeman of New Orleans is not simply a cold case. It is a document of what a city looks like when it is afraid and does not know what it is afraid of.

The attacks happened in a specific community. They happened to people who were already navigating the experience of being immigrants in a country that was not always welcoming. The Italian-American families of the French Quarter were targeted — whether that targeting was deliberate or coincidental remains unknown — and they responded by closing ranks, by not always talking to police, by handling what they could handle internally.

The police investigation, conducted without forensic tools, without centralised records, and in a community that had reasons for its own silence, produced no arrest and no conviction.

The Rosie Cortimiglia episode is perhaps the darkest chapter in the case outside the murders themselves. Two innocent men were nearly executed because a traumatised woman named her personal enemies instead of the person who had actually attacked her. The system failed to question her identification thoroughly enough. It nearly hanged two people for a crime they did not commit.

The Axeman letter sits at the centre of the case not because it solved anything but because it changed what the case was about. Before the letter it was a series of violent crimes. After the letter it was a dialogue between a killer and a city. And the city responded with music.

The killer was never found. The music remained. New Orleans was already the home of jazz before March 19, 1919. After that night it was also the city that played through the night because a killer told it to. The two facts exist together in the historical record. Neither cancels the other out.
