Case File: 026 Organised Crime

The Bosses Voted On The Murder

Published 1 November 2026

How this file was sourced: official records only, listed in Sources below.

◆Part 1: The Vote

The murder was put to a vote. Carmine Persico, boss of the Colombo family, later said that he had voted against it.

The statement was given in evidence by Fred DeChristopher, a cousin of Persico by marriage. The United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit recorded that it provided evidence that the Commission had taken a vote on the matter.

The man the vote was about was Carmine Galante, boss of the Bonanno family. On 12 July 1979 he was having lunch on the terrace of a restaurant in Brooklyn with his cousin Giuseppe Turano and his associate Leonard Coppola. 3 men in ski masks walked in. All 3 diners were shot numerous times at close range.

The court found that the jury could reasonably conclude the Commission had approved the murder of Galante to settle a leadership dispute inside his own family.

On 19 November 1986 a federal jury in the Southern District of New York convicted all 8 defendants of racketeering conspiracy and racketeering. The enterprise was the Commission. 7 of the men were sentenced to 100 years in prison each.

◆Part 2: The Commission

The FBI's history of La Cosa Nostra states that after the murder of Salvatore Maranzano, Charles Luciano set up the Commission to rule all La Cosa Nostra activities. It included bosses from 7 families and divided the rackets among them.

The Second Circuit described what the trial evidence showed it had become. The Commission was the ultimate ruling body over the 5 La Cosa Nostra families in New York City and affiliated families in other cities. The 5 were the Genovese, Gambino, Colombo, Lucchese and Bonanno families.

It coordinated joint criminal ventures between the families. It resolved disputes among them. It recognised the bosses of the families and settled leadership fights inside them. It approved the making of new members. It set the rules.

To be made a member of a family, a candidate had to vow obedience to the rules and orders of the Commission and be personally approved by it. The prior approval of the Commission was required before any family boss could be killed. There was evidence at trial that the Commission had established a death penalty for anyone who murdered a boss without that approval.

From the late 1970s the Commission was controlled by the bosses of 4 of the families. The Bonanno family was denied a seat because of internal instability. During most of the 1970s the Commission directly controlled its operations.

◆Part 3: The Club

The Commission had a scheme in the concrete business. The court called it the Club.

The rules were recorded in the 1989 opinion. Only companies the Commission approved could take concrete construction jobs in New York City worth more than 2 million dollars. Any contractor taking such a job had to pay the Commission 2 percent of the contract price. The Commission decided which company got which job and rigged the bids so that the chosen company submitted the lowest bid.

In exchange the Commission guaranteed labour peace. It enforced the rules by threatened or actual labour unrest or physical harm, even to the point of driving a company out of the concrete business.

The labour peace ran through Ralph Scopo. He was a member of the Colombo family. He was also president and business manager of the District Council of Cement and Concrete Workers, Laborers International Union of North America.

The briefing outline filed with the FBI's announcement of the indictment stated that the Commission formed the Club in the spring of 1981 and that it consisted of approximately 8 concrete companies. The government named 7 of them as victims.

Recorded conversations played at trial showed how the rules were enforced. One company was compelled to pay a penalty because they robbed the Families. Cedar Park Concrete Corp. was forced out of the concrete business because it would no longer make payments. An associate reported to Scopo that he had made North Berry Concrete Corp. pay the money. Another company could take no job without prior approval from a Commission representative.

Officers of 2 concrete companies testified that Scopo and the Commission used the threat of labour unrest, the disruption of concrete supplies and the defendants' reputation for violence to enforce the rules of the Club. At sentencing Judge Richard Owen noted that Scopo's own recorded words showed he had been corrupting organised labour and extorting construction contractors for at least 15 years.

◆Part 4: The Tapes

A great deal of the government's evidence at trial consisted of recorded conversations. Some of the tapes came from inside the Jaguar automobile of Anthony Corallo, boss of the Lucchese family.

In many of the conversations, Commission members and senior family members discussed candidates for made status. They discussed the rules and structure of the Commission. They discussed how to settle disputes between the families. They monitored investigations into their own affairs and the arrests of their co conspirators.

Joseph Bonanno, a former member of the Commission, had made what the court called highly damaging confessions on the television programme 60 Minutes. Corallo, his driver Salvatore Avellino and Salvatore Santoro were recorded assessing what Bonanno had and had not revealed about the Commission.

The FBI's announcement of the indictment stated that in 1979, with expanded use of undercover Special Agents, highly placed informants and electronic intercepts, the FBI had been able to infiltrate the Bonanno family. Joseph Pistone, an undercover FBI agent who successfully infiltrated the Bonanno family, testified at trial and provided recordings.

The briefing outline recorded the scale. More than 175 FBI Special Agents over 4 years. 25 New York City Police Department detectives working daily with the FBI's New York Office. More than 80 Title III electronic intercepts in the investigations of the Gambino, Genovese, Colombo and Bonanno families.

The same outline listed the immunized testimony of LCN Boss Joseph Bonanno among the proofs used in the case. It was marked with an asterisk. The note read: This information should not be made public.

◆Part 5: The Hit

The Second Circuit, sitting in banc, set out how the Galante murder was planned. The Commission worked through Aniello Dellacroce, underboss of the Gambino family, Stefano Canone, consigliere of the Bonanno family, and Bonanno soldiers including Anthony Indelicato, Dominic Trinchera and Cesare Bonventre.

In May or June 1979 Trinchera introduced Indelicato to Louis Giongetti, a lifelong felon. Indelicato asked Giongetti whether he had ever hit anyone. Giongetti assured him that he was experienced and trustworthy. In early July Trinchera sent Giongetti to a safe house to collect a cache of shotguns and other weapons.

On 12 July 1979 Indelicato and 2 other men in ski masks entered the restaurant and shot and killed Galante, Turano and Coppola. The evidence showed that 2 other men who had come to lunch with Coppola, one of them Bonventre, were uninjured and joined in the shooting.

The killers fled in a car stolen a month earlier. Indelicato's palm print was on an inside door handle of the getaway car. Within half an hour he was at a social club in Manhattan with his father, his uncle and another Bonanno capo, Philip Giaccone, reporting to Dellacroce and Canone. A surveillance videotape showed Canone congratulating him.

There was evidence that the penalty for killing a boss without Commission approval was death. Indelicato was not killed. He, Trinchera and Bonventre were promoted to capo.

After the murder the Commission reorganised the Bonanno family under Philip Rastelli and returned autonomous control to the family for the first time in a decade. Rastelli had been Galante's rival for the leadership. He was in jail when Galante was killed.

◆Part 6: The Indictment

On 26 February 1985 FBI Director Webster announced the indictment of 9 men as the Commission of La Cosa Nostra. The list named Anthony Salerno, boss of the Genovese family. Paul Castellano, boss of the Gambino family, and his underboss Aniello Dellacroce. Gennaro Langella, acting boss of the Colombo family. Anthony Corallo, boss of the Lucchese family, with his underboss Salvatore Santoro and consigliere Christopher Furnari. Philip Rastelli, boss of the Bonanno family. Ralph Scopo, a soldier in the Colombo family.

The charge was racketeering, with predicate offences of extortion, murder and labour racketeering. Webster said that for the first time the national structure of La Cosa Nostra was revealed through the defendants' own words and actions.

Carmine Persico was a fugitive from November 1984 to February 1985. He hid at the home of Fred DeChristopher. The court found that the house had become his base of operations. Persico had to tell DeChristopher who to trust, who could come to the house and who held which positions in the family.

The copy of the FBI announcement held by the National Archives sits in the files of John G. Roberts at the Ronald Reagan Presidential Library. It is filed behind a 1 line note to John Roberts from the Office of the Deputy Attorney General, dated 26 February 1985. Per our conversation.

By trial the list had changed. Dellacroce and Canone died of natural causes in 1985. Rastelli was severed because he was on trial in another case in Brooklyn. Castellano was murdered before trial. On 19 November 1985 Indelicato was indicted on 2 racketeering counts. The only acts charged against him were the 3 murders of 12 July 1979. The 8 men tried were Salerno, Persico, Langella, Corallo, Santoro, Scopo, Furnari and Indelicato.

◆Part 7: The Verdict

The trial began on 8 September 1986 before Judge Richard Owen in the Southern District of New York. It lasted 11 weeks. The jury was sequestered only for its 5 days of deliberation.

Witnesses included a former acting boss of the Cleveland family, 3 law enforcement witnesses who described an organised crime meeting in New York State in 1957, a former member of the Colombo family who had recorded conversations, Fred DeChristopher and the undercover agent Joseph Pistone.

On 19 November 1986, a year to the day after Indelicato was indicted, the jury returned guilty verdicts against all defendants.

Salerno, Persico, Langella, Corallo, Santoro, Scopo and Furnari were convicted of racketeering conspiracy, racketeering, conspiracy to commit extortion and 12 counts of extortion or attempted extortion. Each was also convicted of 6 labour bribery violations. Corallo and Santoro were convicted of conspiracy to make extortionate extensions of credit. Indelicato was convicted on both racketeering counts. The jury found he had committed the 3 murders.

Each of the 7 was sentenced to a total of 100 years. Indelicato received 40 years and a fine of 50,000 dollars. Corallo and Santoro were each fined 250,000 dollars. The others were each fined 240,000 dollars.

The 100 years were built from consecutive 20 year terms. The court of appeals noted that had Judge Owen not made the other sentences concurrent, they could have received more than 300 years.

◆Part 8: The Record

Indelicato argued that 3 murders committed in a single episode could not be a pattern of racketeering. On 13 January 1989 the Second Circuit sitting in banc rejected the argument.

On 31 January 1989 the Second Circuit affirmed the convictions of all 8 men on every count but 1. Indelicato's substantive racketeering conviction was reversed because all of his acts took place before 19 November 1980, outside the 5 year limit. His racketeering conspiracy conviction stood.

The defendants argued that their sentences violated the Eighth Amendment and that the identical 100 year terms were mechanistic. The court admitted the sentences had a certain symbolic quality. It found no abuse of discretion and upheld them.

Persico represented himself on the appeal.

The bosses ruled the families. They made the members. They took 2 percent of the concrete. They voted on a murder and promoted one of the men who carried it out.
