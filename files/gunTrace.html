Case File: 031 Government & Corruption

The Heroin Came From The Police Car

Published 22 November 2026

How this file was sourced: official records only, listed in Sources below.

◆Part 1: The Stuff

On 28 April 2010 a Baltimore police officer named Wayne Jenkins chased a car through the northwest of the city. He was driving an unmarked police vehicle. Umar Burley was driving the car. Brent Matthews was his passenger.

At the intersection of Belle Avenue and Gwyn Oak Avenue, Burley's car struck a car entering the intersection. The impact pushed that car onto the front porch of a row house. Its elderly driver was trapped inside and died later that day. The independent investigation later commissioned for the city named him as Elbert Davis Sr.

Jenkins did not find any drugs in Burley's car.

After the 2 men were arrested, Jenkins told another officer to call a sergeant who was not at the scene, because he had the stuff in his car. Later an officer found approximately 28 grams of heroin in Burley's car. In his plea agreement Jenkins admitted that the heroin had come from his own police vehicle and had been planted.

That day Jenkins wrote a Statement of Probable Cause. It said 32 individually wrapped pieces of suspected high purity heroin had been recovered from the car. He signed it under penalties of perjury.

Jenkins listened to the 2 men's recorded jail calls. They were saying the heroin had been planted on them. He told a fellow officer he could not testify if the case went to trial, because something had been put in the car.

Burley and Matthews were charged with federal drug offences and imprisoned. Matthews was held until 9 September 2013. Burley was held until 31 August 2017. Jenkins did not correct his statement while either man was in prison.

◆Part 2: The Unit

The Gun Trace Task Force was created in 2007. Jenkins's plea agreement set out its mission: to track and trace recovered firearms and to suppress the possession, purchase and trafficking of illegal guns in Baltimore.

The independent investigation found that by 2012 the unit no longer bore any resemblance to that mission. Its members no longer spent any significant time on ammunition logs or straw purchaser cases. Despite its name, it had become just another street enforcement unit.

Jenkins joined the Baltimore Police Department on 20 February 2003. The investigation found that he was notorious for reckless driving, and that Commissioner Frederick Bealefeld denied him promotion to sergeant for as long as Bealefeld held the post. After Bealefeld left, Jenkins was promoted on 30 November 2012.

He took charge of a Special Enforcement Section unit on 14 October 2013. He took charge of the Gun Trace Task Force on 13 June 2016. 3 detectives from his old unit went with him: Evodio Hendrix, Marcus Taylor and Maurice Ward.

His plea agreement records that he told the people he robbed that he was a federal task force officer. He was not. He told his officers to introduce him as the US Attorney. He was not that either.

◆Part 3: The Robberies

Jenkins admitted taking part in 7 robberies between May 2011 and August 2016, among others. He admitted that officers carried their police service firearms during them, that some victims were physically restrained, and that he wrote or approved false reports to hide them.

On 11 May 2011 a man fled a traffic stop, crashed his car and ran. While another detective chased him, Jenkins stayed with the car. He stole at least 1,800 dollars from it.

On 11 July 2014 a man fled officers in the parking lot of Mondawmin Mall. He jumped over a retaining wall and was injured in the fall. Jenkins and Hendrix stole between 12,000 and 14,000 dollars from his car. They divided it in the parking lot of the department's Western District.

In the spring of 2015 Jenkins, Taylor and Ward interrupted a large marijuana sale at the Belvedere Towers apartments. Jenkins took a bag holding 30 pounds of marijuana from the seller and a bag holding 15,000 dollars from the buyer. He told them he was a DEA agent and let them go.

He then led Taylor and Ward to a wooded area near Northern Parkway and Liberty Road. He told them to leave their phones in the car. Out of sight of the road he gave each of them about 5,000 dollars. He kept the rest. An associate sold the marijuana for 2,000 dollars a pound and gave Jenkins 30,000 dollars.

◆Part 4: The Safe

On 22 March 2016 Jenkins, Taylor, Hendrix and Ward arrested Oreese Stevenson after a traffic stop. They took his house key and read his address from his driving licence.

The Court of Appeals recorded the trial evidence of what came next. The officers went to his house and did what they called a sneak and peek. A former officer described it to the jury: going into a home without anybody knowing and searching it without a search warrant.

Inside they found cocaine and a safe. Some officers left to get a search warrant. When they came back with it, they opened the safe. It held 200,000 dollars. They took 100,000 dollars for themselves and reported that 100,000 dollars had been found.

Jenkins admitted that he also stole 2 kilograms of cocaine from the house. He called his associate to the street outside during the search and handed it over. The associate sold it over time for 30,000 dollars and gave Jenkins 15,000.

Afterwards the 4 officers went to Taylor's house. In the basement Jenkins gave Taylor, Hendrix and Ward at least 20,000 dollars each. He kept at least 20,000 dollars for himself. A Breitling wristwatch valued at 4,000 dollars was also taken from the house.

Jenkins admitted that the unit entered homes without a warrant on multiple occasions to look for guns. When guns were found, the officers then obtained a warrant. They lied in the affidavit about the probable cause, or did not disclose that they had already been inside.

◆Part 5: The US Attorney

On 8 July 2016 detectives Jemell Rayam, Momodu Gondo and Daniel Hersl stopped Ronald and Nancy Hamilton and put them in handcuffs. At 3:11 in the afternoon Gondo called Jenkins.

The plea agreement quotes the call. Jenkins asked whether Gondo had told them anything. Gondo said no. Jenkins said: Just tell them you gotta wait for the U.S. Attorney. He told Gondo to introduce him as the US Attorney when he arrived.

Jenkins told Ronald Hamilton he was a federal officer. Hamilton said he had money at home. The officers took the couple to their house in custody and found 70,000 dollars in cash. Before Maryland State Police arrived to execute a search warrant, they took 20,000 dollars. The state police recovered the other 50,000 dollars.

No drugs, firearms or other evidence of crime were found at the house. Neither of the Hamiltons was charged with any crime. Ronald Hamilton testified that the cash came from his used car business and from gambling.

That evening Jenkins, Hersl, Gondo and Rayam met at a bar in Baltimore and split 17,000 dollars of the stolen money. Jenkins also stole Ronald Hamilton's wristwatch. Rayam wrote the incident report and certified it as true. Jenkins approved it. It did not mention the 20,000 dollars.

◆Part 6: The Shed

Jenkins admitted that he took drugs from people he detained and gave them to an associate to sell. He brought stolen drugs to the associate's home several times a week. The visits became so frequent that the associate gave him a key to his shed, so Jenkins could leave drugs there at any hour of the day or night. In total the associate paid Jenkins 200,000 to 250,000 dollars.

Jenkins also stole about 12 pounds of high grade marijuana that law enforcement had intercepted in the US mail. In April 2015, after the riots that followed the death of Freddie Gray, he brought the associate prescription medicines stolen from someone looting a pharmacy.

On 8 August 2016 Jenkins and Hersl arrested a man they had watched leaving a storage facility. While 2 other officers went to prepare a search warrant for the man's storage unit, Jenkins called the associate and told him to break into it. The associate took about three quarters of a kilogram of cocaine. Jenkins shared the proceeds with Hersl.

Jenkins put an illegal GPS tracking device on a suspected drug dealer's car. He followed it to a Sam's Club parking lot and sent the associate to break into it. The associate took 15,000 to 20,000 dollars. In a separate break in, Jenkins and the associate stole 7 luxury watches, including 5 Rolexes, worth 170,000 to 200,000 dollars.

On 3 August 2018 the US Attorney's Office announced a 5 year prison sentence for Donald Stepp, a bail bondsman. It said Jenkins brought Stepp to search locations and falsely told other law enforcement agencies that Stepp was a police officer. Stepp stored drugs stolen by Jenkins in his tool shed, sold them and returned hundreds of thousands of dollars in cash to the officers.

◆Part 7: The Overtime

Jenkins admitted that he routinely submitted false overtime reports. He certified that he had worked his full shift and extra hours. He had not worked all of them, and in some cases any of them.

As supervisor he approved false overtime for his officers. He told them when to arrive, in many cases hours after the regular shift began, and how much overtime to claim. When some of the unit made a gun arrest, every member submitted overtime as if they had taken part. On some occasions none of them was working at all on the day of the arrest.

The 4 detectives who pleaded guilty and cooperated also admitted overtime fraud. The independent investigation recorded that this included times when they were out of town and on vacation.

The Court of Appeals recorded that Taylor's and Hersl's overtime pay during the period almost matched their base salaries. It doubled their pay. The slips went to a timekeeper, then into an ADP payroll system on servers in Sioux Falls, South Dakota, and then in most cases straight into the officers' bank accounts. The court held that this was enough to make the overtime claims wire fraud, one of the crimes behind their racketeering convictions.

◆Part 8: The BB Guns

Jenkins told his officers about the 2010 heroin. He discussed carrying BB gun pistols so that he could plant one on a suspect if he could not find a real firearm. When the unit was arrested, BB gun pistols were recovered from their vehicles.

The independent investigation found it had already happened once. On 26 March 2014 Jenkins struck Demetric Simon with his car after a pursuit. Simon was on foot. He was not armed. Jenkins called his longtime mentor, Keith Gladstone, and a BB gun was planted at the scene. In 2019 Gladstone pleaded guilty to conspiracy to violate Simon's civil rights by planting it.

At the time the department took no action against Jenkins, not even for the use of his vehicle. Its new Use of Force Review Board found that use justified and within policy.

A month earlier Jenkins had stopped Walter Price's car and claimed to find cocaine. Internal Affairs sustained several charges against him. Demotion, a transfer back to patrol and a lengthy suspension were proposed. The sanction was reduced to non punitive counselling.

In 2016 Jenkins learned from sources inside the department and from an Assistant State's Attorney that members of his unit were under federal investigation. In about June 2016 a police officer told him there was a federal wiretap on Gondo's phone. He told Hersl. The officers kept committing crimes.

◆Part 9: The Sentences

The case did not start inside the department. The independent investigation found that 2 county police departments working a narcotics case found a tracking device illegally placed on a target's vehicle. It led to a referral to an FBI task force. None of the officers' colleagues had reported any of them to Internal Affairs or the FBI.

On 1 March 2017, 7 members of the Gun Trace Task Force were arrested: Jenkins, Gondo, Hendrix, Hersl, Rayam, Taylor and Ward. The federal indictment charged that they had turned the unit, and the department as a whole, into a racketeering enterprise. In the Howard County Detention Center, Jenkins told the others to keep their mouths shut and stick to the story.

Gondo, Hendrix, Rayam and Ward pleaded guilty and testified. On 5 January 2018 Jenkins pleaded guilty to racketeering conspiracy, racketeering, 2 robberies, falsifying the 2010 Statement of Probable Cause and 4 counts of depriving Burley and Matthews of their rights under colour of law. On 12 February 2018 a jury convicted Taylor and Hersl of racketeering and robbery. Each was sentenced to 18 years. The Court of Appeals upheld their convictions on 5 November 2019.

On 7 June 2018 Judge Catherine Blake sentenced Jenkins to 25 years: 20 years for the racketeering and robberies and 5 consecutive years for the 2010 heroin. She said she did not know the exact number of cases affected, and cited a recent estimate from the Baltimore City State's Attorney of 1,700. She said there was no denying that hundreds of criminal cases had had to be dismissed because the officers' credibility had been destroyed. She called it a great abuse of the public trust.

Jenkins spoke before he was sentenced. He said: I'm so sorry to the citizens of Baltimore. Mr. Umar Burley, God forgive me. I wish I would have came clean when I found out that drugs were planted.

The independent report, released in January 2022, counted 13 defendants charged from the federal investigation. It recorded that Ward and Hendrix were sentenced to 7 years each, Gondo to 10 years and Rayam to 12 years.

On 10 March 2023 the Court of Appeals upheld an order that Taylor pay 228,304 dollars in restitution to 2 people he and his confederates had robbed.

The heroin came from the police car. The officer who swore it came from Burley's car went on to run the Gun Trace Task Force.
