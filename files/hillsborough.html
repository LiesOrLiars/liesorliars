The Police Altered The Statements

Published 8 November 2026

How this file was sourced: official records only, listed in Sources below.

◆Part 1: The Deletion

An officer wrote: I at no time heard any directions being given in terms of leadership. The sentence was deleted from the statement.

It was 1 of 116. The Report of the Hillsborough Independent Panel, published on 12 September 2012, found that 194 statements by South Yorkshire Police officers were identified for amendment. 164 were marked for amendments more substantial than simple corrections. 116 of those 164 were amended to remove or alter comments unfavourable to the force.

41 of them downplayed or removed criticisms officers had made of their own leadership and of the police response to the disaster.

Another officer had written that even with a number of higher ranks in the area nobody seemed to be organising the injured. That was deleted too.

The statements described 15 April 1989. They were altered before they went to the public inquiry.

◆Part 2: The Crush

On 15 April 1989 Liverpool played Nottingham Forest in an FA Cup semi final at Hillsborough Stadium, Sheffield, the home of Sheffield Wednesday. The Panel recorded that over 50,000 people travelled to the match.

The Interim Report of Lord Justice Taylor, presented to Parliament in August 1989, set out what happened at the Leppings Lane end. Crowds built up outside the turnstiles. Superintendent Roger Marshall, in command outside, asked 3 times for the exit gates to be opened. He said that if they were not, someone was going to be killed.

In the police control room the match commander, Chief Superintendent David Duckenfield, gave the order. At 2.52pm gate C was opened wide. In the 5 minutes it was open about 2000 fans passed through it. A large proportion headed straight for the tunnel in front of them. The tunnel led to central pens 3 and 4.

Only 6 minutes into the game, play was stopped. Taylor recorded that 95 died and over 400 received hospital treatment.

The Panel recorded 96 deaths. The High Court explained the difference in 2012. 95 spectators died that afternoon. Anthony Bland was left in a persistent vegetative state by his injuries and died in March 1993.

Taylor found that the immediate cause was the failure, when gate C was opened, to cut off access to the central pens, which were already overfull. He wrote that the main reason for the disaster was the failure of police control.

65 police officers gave oral evidence to his inquiry. Taylor reported that for the most part the quality of their evidence was in inverse proportion to their rank. With some notable exceptions, the senior officers in command were defensive and evasive witnesses.

◆Part 3: The First Story

The match commander had been appointed 21 days earlier. The Panel found that South Yorkshire Police replaced its experienced commander, Chief Superintendent Brian Mole, with Chief Superintendent Duckenfield. Duckenfield had minimal experience of policing at Hillsborough. None of the documents disclosed to the Panel gave the reason.

As the severity of the disaster became apparent, the Panel found, Duckenfield told a falsehood to senior officials. He said Liverpool fans had broken into the stadium and caused an inrush into the central pens. The allegation was broadcast internationally. It was the first explanation of the disaster to enter the public domain.

The report of the IOPC and Operation Resolve investigations, published on 2 December 2025, states that Duckenfield has since acknowledged those comments were lies. At the Goldring Inquests in 2015 he apologised unreservedly to the families of those who died.

On the morning after the disaster, South Yorkshire Police documents disclosed to the Panel show, senior officers discussed privately the animalistic behaviour of drunken marauding fans. They agreed not to make it a public issue in case they were perceived as avoiding responsibility.

On 19 April 1989 the Chief Constable joined a meeting of Police Federation members in Sheffield. The Panel recorded his advice. The force case had to be pulled together and given to the Inquiry. A defence had to be prepared and a rock solid story presented. He considered that blame should be directed towards drunken ticketless individuals.

◆Part 4: The Recollections

The process began the day after the disaster. At a South Yorkshire Police briefing on Sunday 16 April 1989 the record states: Duty statements are out.

Officers were told not to write up their pocket books. The briefing officer said: Do not start making pocket book entries.

On 17 April Chief Constable Peter Wright agreed that all officers at the game make records of their recollections. He said that if the force left it to West Midlands Police to provide the evidence, they might not get the broad scope of evidence flowing in. The notes record that South Yorkshire Police should be the authors of most of the information fed in.

The recollections were to be written by officers themselves on plain paper. They would not be taken under Criminal Justice Act rules.

On 26 April the force asked its counsel, Bill Woodward QC, whether that was acceptable. He replied: It couldn't be better. They can put all the things in that they want and we will sort them out.

The force solicitors were Hammond Suddards. Peter Metcalf, a senior partner, agreed to vet the accounts. A note to the force incident room on 10 May set the rule. Nothing currently in our possession will be released to W/Mids until it has been vetted by our legal representatives.

◆Part 5: The Edits

The work was run by a small team under Chief Superintendent Donald Denton. The Panel recorded that hundreds of recollections were vetted, continuing into June 1989.

The stated purpose was to remove conjecture and opinion and leave only matters of fact. The disclosed correspondence showed more. Comments unhelpful to the force case were altered, deleted or qualified. An early draft by Metcalf proposed that any statement criticising other officers, senior or junior, should not be allowed to stand.

The Panel found that officers were discouraged from criticising the responses of senior officers. Words such as chaotic were counselled against. If included, they were deleted.

The High Court quoted 1 statement in 2012. It originally read: Certain supervisory officers were conspicuous by their absence. It was utter chaos. After alteration it read: On the pitch were Chief Inspectors Beale and Sumner and Superintendent Greenwood. The court recorded that references to chaos, fear, panic and confusion made by 23 officers were altered or deleted.

Counsel to the Taylor Inquiry took the view that there was absolutely no reason for excluding such expressions of opinion where they touched on matters relevant to the inquiry. The Panel found the process was not considered improper and no objection was raised.

The alterations became public 8 years after the disaster. One officer told the judicial scrutiny of new evidence that he had accepted the changes only because he was suffering from depression and post traumatic stress. He called it an injustice for statements to have been doctored to suit the management of South Yorkshire Police.

◆Part 6: The Second Count

The IOPC and Operation Resolve report of 2 December 2025 counted again.

It found that 426 accounts were sent to Hammond Suddards for review. The lawyers, predominantly Metcalf, suggested amendments to 172 of them. The first faxes back from the solicitors suggested changes to 28 of 68 accounts. The IOPC found no evidence that Denton or any member of his team challenged them. The suggestions were treated as instructions and acted upon.

Amended versions were retyped and sent back to the officers to sign. They were not necessarily told what had been changed.

In total 327 South Yorkshire Police officer accounts were amended. The IOPC stated that this was 133 more than identified in the Panel report and approximately 1 in 4 of all the force officer accounts relating to the day. 297 of the amended accounts went to West Midlands Police.

One suggestion read: I would suggest substitution of the words extreme urgency for panic.

References to police contingency plans to close the tunnel to the central pens were removed from all 5 accounts in which they appeared. Comments indicating that the force had any responsibility for monitoring safety in the pens were removed from all 8 accounts where they appeared.

West Midlands Police only received the amended accounts. The IOPC found that its investigation was based on the information South Yorkshire Police chose to give it.

◆Part 7: The Cut Off

The first inquests opened on 19 April 1989. The coroner imposed a cut off. The inquiry into events that afternoon never extended beyond 3.15pm.

The Panel found that 3.15pm was chosen because it was an undisputed and recorded time when an ambulance arrived on the pitch. The coroner rounded the time to the nearest quarter hour. The rationale was that all who died had suffered fatal and irreversible injuries by then.

The Panel reviewed every post mortem report. 28 of those who died did not have traumatic asphyxia with obstruction of the blood circulation. In 31 the heart and lungs had continued to function after the crush, in 16 of them for a prolonged period. The Panel found it could not be concluded that life or death was inevitably determined by events before 3.15pm.

The coroner took blood alcohol samples from all of the deceased. The disclosed documents gave no rationale for that decision. Police National Computer checks were carried out on those with a non zero alcohol level. The Panel found no evidence that alcohol played any part in the genesis of the disaster.

On 28 March 1991 the jury at the first inquests returned a verdict of accidental death in each case.

◆Part 8: The Quashing

On 19 December 2012 the High Court gave judgment on an application by the Attorney General under section 13 of the Coroners Act 1988.

On the altered statements the court said the relevance to the cause of death was not immediately obvious. It said it was open to a new inquest to consider whether the efforts made by some of the authorities to conceal evidence relating to neglect and breach of duty may have some relevant bearing on the cause of death.

The order was recorded in 2 sentences. All the inquisitions will be quashed. There will be new inquests in each and every case.

◆Part 9: The Determinations

The fresh inquests opened in Warrington on 31 March 2014 before Sir John Goldring. The jury heard 296 days of evidence.

On 26 April 2016 the jury answered 14 general questions. It agreed that 96 people died due to crushing in the central pens of the Leppings Lane terrace, following the admission of a large number of supporters through exit gates.

It found errors or omissions in police planning, in policing at the turnstiles, by the commanding officers and by the commanding officers in the control box when the exit gates were opened. It wrote that commanding officers failed to recognise that pens 3 and 4 were at capacity before gate C was opened, and failed to order the closure of the tunnel.

Question 6 asked whether those who died were unlawfully killed. The legal directions required the jury to be sure that Duckenfield owed a duty of care, breached it, that the breach caused the deaths, and that it amounted to gross negligence. The answer was yes, by a majority of 7 to 2.

Question 7 asked whether any behaviour by football supporters caused or contributed to the dangerous situation at the Leppings Lane turnstiles. The answer was no. Asked whether it may have, the answer was no.

The jury also found errors or omissions by the ambulance service and in the safety certification of the stadium. It found that the stadium engineers should have done more to detect and advise on unsafe features. The Home Secretary told Parliament that in all but 1 case the jury recorded a time bracket for death running beyond 3.15pm.

The statements were altered in 1989. The verdict of accidental death stood until 2012. In 2016 the jury found the 96 were unlawfully killed.
