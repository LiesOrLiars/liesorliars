The Landlady

◆Part 1: The Girl

Her name was Dorothea Helen Gray. She called herself many things.

Born on January 9, 1929, in Redlands, California, the sixth of seven children. Both parents were alcoholics. Her father held a gun to his head in front of his children. Repeatedly. As a form of communication.

He died of tuberculosis when she was 8. Her mother lost custody of all the children in 1938 and died in a motorcycle accident before the year was out. Both parents gone before she was 10. The state took the children and placed them in an orphanage where she was sexually abused.

She was 9 years old.

By the time she was 19 she had been orphaned, institutionalised, married, abandoned and had given away both of her children. She had also been arrested for check forgery.

She had not yet begun.

◆Part 2: The Persona

The thing about Dorothea Puente is that she understood, earlier than most people ever do, that identity is a performance.

She married a merchant seaman in 1952 and invented herself entirely. She called herself Teya Singoalla Neyaarda. A Muslim woman of Egyptian and Israeli descent. She was a white woman from Redlands, California. It didn't matter. She committed to it completely.

She lied her entire life whether or not it made strategic sense. She lied big and small. The number of children she had changed constantly. She once told a reporter she had been a Rockette.

She was arrested in 1960 for running a bookkeeping firm as a front for a brothel. Briefly committed to a psychiatric hospital in 1961. Then she married again. And again. And somewhere in the middle of it all she landed in Sacramento and decided to become a pillar of the community.

She was very good at becoming what the room needed her to be. This would turn out to be the most dangerous thing about her.

◆Part 3: The House

The boarding house at 1426 F Street was a blue and white Victorian on a tree-lined street in Sacramento. Her front yard had roses. Well-tended flowers.

Social workers looked upon Dorothea Puente and her boarding house with admiration. She had a reputation for taking in people considered tough cases. Recovering alcoholics. Drug addicts. The mentally ill. The elderly.

Beginning in 1986, social worker Peggy Nickerson alone sent 19 clients to Puente's home.

While they were living there, she read their mail and took any money and Social Security checks they received.

She had done this before. She lost her first boarding house after being caught signing her own name to tenants' benefit checks. Convicted. Sentenced to 5 years. Released after 3. A state psychologist diagnosed her as a schizophrenic with no remorse who should be closely monitored.

Instead, she opened the boarding house on F Street. Puente's house was visited by several parole agents. Despite these frequent visits, she was never charged with anything. They kept coming. They kept leaving.

◆Part 4: The Method

She used Dalmane. A sedative. All the buried bodies were found to contain traces of it.

She didn't stab anyone. She didn't use violence in the way people expect when they hear the word murder. She made tea. She served meals. She was a small, white-haired woman in large glasses who called her tenants by name and remembered their prescriptions and tucked people in at night.

And then she adjusted the dosage.

When someone disappeared, Puente told the other tenants the person had moved out, or that she had thrown them out for excessive drinking. No one outside the house really noticed.

That last part is the one that should sit with you.

These were people on the margins. Mentally ill. Elderly. Disabled. Homeless before they arrived at her door. They lived mostly solitary existences. In some cases they were forgotten while they were still alive, and their deaths could have easily gone unnoticed, as Puente had hoped.

She had chosen them because of exactly that. The invisible are easier to disappear.

◆Part 5: The Discovery

In November 1988 a tenant named Alvaro Montoya disappeared. He was developmentally disabled and had schizophrenia. He had been homeless for years before being referred to Puente's house because of her sterling reputation.

A social worker noticed Montoya had gone missing. She didn't buy Puente's explanation. She pushed. She kept pushing.

Neighbours had reported the smell of rotting flesh from the vicinity.

Police arrived. They found recently disturbed soil. They started digging. They uncovered 7 bodies in the yard.

While they were digging, Puente was inside the house. Cooperative. Calm. The frail grandmother. She asked if she could go for a walk to clear her head.

They said yes. She could go for a walk. She fled to Los Angeles. The man she began talking to in a bar recognised her from the news and called the police.

◆Part 6: The Trial and What It Revealed

She was charged with the murders of 9 people. The trial began in 1993. It lasted 5 months.

The jury deadlocked on 6 of the counts, in part because of her grandmotherly disposition. The woman in the dock did not match the crime. That was the trick she had been running her entire life and it worked even here, at the end, in a courtroom with 7 bodies already in the ground.

She was convicted of 3 murders and received back-to-back life sentences. She maintained her innocence until she died.

Here is what was true at the same time the bodies were being dug up: Puente had a prior conviction for drugging and robbing elderly victims. Explicit release conditions said no contact with elderly people. A state psychologist had found a woman with no remorse who should be closely monitored. Instead, she opened her second boarding house. Parole agents visited repeatedly. They kept leaving.

She had built her entire operation on the gap in a system that had already decided her victims were hard to help. Once they were housed, the responsibility felt discharged. Nobody was checking whether they were still alive.

Dorothea Puente died in 2011 at the Central California Women's Facility. She was 82. She never stopped performing.

The condition she found and exploited still exists.
