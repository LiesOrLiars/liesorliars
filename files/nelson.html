The Gorilla Man: Earle Nelson

◆Part 1: The Beginning

Earle Leonard Nelson was born on May 12, 1897, in San Francisco. His mother died of syphilis when he was less than a year old. His father died of the same disease two years later. He was raised by his maternal grandmother, Jennie Nelson, a strict religious woman who read the Bible aloud to him regularly and maintained a household that was organised around scripture and discipline.

At age 10, Earle was hit by a streetcar. He was unconscious for six days. When he woke he had a dent in his skull above his left temple and a changed personality that nobody around him had the clinical vocabulary to describe. He became prone to violent outbursts. He began exposing himself to neighbourhood children. He lost and found employment repeatedly.

By his teens he had developed a habit of quoting scripture at length and without apparent context, carrying a Bible constantly, and presenting himself in social situations as a quiet and deeply religious man.

The Bible was real. The religion was real, in the sense that it had been absorbed so thoroughly during childhood that it had become part of his presentation to the world. Whether the faith beneath the presentation was real is a question that cannot be answered now and probably could not have been answered then.

◆Part 2: The First Crimes

In 1918, Earle Nelson was arrested for attempting to sexually assault a young girl in the basement of a San Francisco building. He was 21 years old. He was examined by psychiatrists and found to be insane. He was committed to the Napa State Hospital.

He escaped within the year. He was recaptured. He escaped again. He was institutionalised repeatedly across the early 1920s.

In 1919 he married Mary Fuller. The marriage was brief and troubled. She later described him as strange and violent. She told people he read the Bible constantly and that she had been frightened of him.

The psychiatric examinations of Nelson during this period noted his religious obsession, his history of assault, and his general instability. None of them resulted in any long-term institutional commitment.

By 1926, Earle Nelson was 29 years old. He had a documented history of sexual violence, multiple escapes from psychiatric custody, a failed marriage, and a series of low-wage jobs that he never held for long. He was carrying a Bible and moving through a country that had no mechanism to hold him.

The first murder he is believed to have committed occurred on February 20, 1926, in San Francisco.

◆Part 3: San Francisco, February 1926

Clara Newman was 60 years old. She ran a rooming house in San Francisco and had placed an advertisement for a vacant room.

A polite young man with a Bible came to enquire about the room. He was neatly dressed. He spoke quietly and seemed sincere. He told her he was a religious man looking for a clean, respectable place to stay.

She showed him the room. He strangled her with his hands.

Her body was found hidden in the house by her nephew Theodore Newman, who had come to visit. She had been sexually assaulted after death.

The San Francisco Police Department investigated. They had no leads, no witnesses, and no description of the visitor beyond what the neighbourhood could provide. A quiet young man with a Bible had knocked on the door. Clara Newman had answered it.

Within four months, Earle Nelson had killed three more women in San Francisco and San Jose. The method was always the same. He found boarding house advertisements. He presented himself as a quiet, religious man seeking a room. He strangled the woman showing him the room. He hid the body.

The attacks were not yet connected. Each was investigated as a separate crime. Each investigation went nowhere.

◆Part 4: The Method

Earle Nelson's method was simple and devastatingly effective.

Boarding houses in the 1920s were a standard form of accommodation across the United States. A single woman running a boarding house was entirely normal. She would place an advertisement in the local newspaper, take enquiries from strangers, and show rooms to people she had never met before. This was not carelessness. This was business.

Nelson understood this. He found the advertisements in newspapers. He appeared at the door at a reasonable hour, dressed reasonably, carrying his Bible. He was polite. He asked sensible questions about the room, the price, the house rules. He was shown the room.

Then he strangled the woman showing it to him.

The Bible was not incidental to the method. It served as a signal — to the landlady, to any neighbours who saw him arrive, to anyone who might later be asked to describe the visitor. A man with a Bible did not need to be feared. A man with a Bible was the kind of man you opened your door to.

Nelson moved constantly. After each murder he relocated. He never stayed in one city long enough for the pattern to be identified before he was already somewhere else. He killed in San Francisco, San Jose, Santa Barbara, Oakland, Portland, Seattle, San Diego, Council Bluffs, Chicago, Philadelphia, Buffalo, Detroit, and finally Winnipeg.

He covered the continent in 18 months.

◆Part 5: The Escalation

By the end of 1926, Nelson had killed at least 9 women. By the spring of 1927 the count had reached 16.

The murders were occurring faster. The distances between them were shorter. He was moving through the Pacific Northwest and into the Midwest with increasing speed, as if the intervals between killings were compressing.

In Portland, Oregon, he killed three women in three weeks. In Council Bluffs, Iowa, he killed a 23-year-old woman named Mary McConnell on December 23, 1926, and an 8-year-old girl named Lola Belle Cowell on the same day. The murder of the child was a departure from the established pattern. It was the only child victim attributed to Nelson.

The murders were now connected in the public mind. Newspapers were running stories about a killer moving through boarding houses across the country. He was acquiring nicknames — the Dark Strangler, the Gorilla Man. The second nickname came from the unusual heaviness of his jaw and brow, described by witnesses who had seen him.

The connection between the murders had been made. What had not been made was a connection to a specific man. Police departments across multiple states and cities were looking for a description that could have matched thousands of men.

◆Part 6: Canada

On June 8, 1927, Earle Nelson crossed the border into Canada at Emerson, Manitoba. He told the border officer he was a labourer looking for work.

On June 9, he rented a room from a woman named Emily Patterson in Winnipeg. He strangled her and hid her body under a bed. Her husband William came home that evening to find the house quiet and his wife missing. He found her the following morning.

On June 10, Nelson rented a room from a 14-year-old girl named Lola Cowan, whose parents were away. He strangled her and hid her body under the bed in the room he had rented.

Winnipeg police now had two murders in two days. Both involved a rooming house. Both involved strangulation. Both involved a body hidden under a bed.

The Canadian authorities were in contact with American police departments that had been hunting the same man. The description matched. The method matched. The Bible matched.

Nelson was already moving. He had taken a train west toward Regina.

◆Part 7: The Watch

On June 11, 1927, Earle Nelson was in the small town of Wakopa, Manitoba. He tried to sell a pocket watch to a local businessman. The watch had belonged to William Patterson, the husband of his Winnipeg victim. Nelson had taken it after killing Emily Patterson.

The businessman did not recognise the watch, but he recognised the description that had been circulating through the province. A man matching the description of the boarding house killer was trying to sell jewellery in a small Manitoba town.

He contacted the local authorities.

Nelson was arrested on June 15, 1927, in Killarney, Manitoba. He had been on the run for less than a week in Canada. The watch had cost him everything.

He gave the name Virgil Wilson when arrested. Officers recognised him from the circulating description. He was taken to Winnipeg and formally identified.

He was 30 years old. He had murdered at least 22 people across the United States and Canada in 18 months. He had been caught because he tried to sell a stolen watch for 50 cents.

◆Part 8: The Trial

Earle Nelson was tried in Winnipeg for the murders of Emily Patterson and Lola Cowan. The trial began on November 1, 1927, and lasted six days.

The defence entered a plea of not guilty by reason of insanity. The history was substantial — the childhood head injury, the multiple psychiatric commitments, the documented institutional assessment of mental instability across multiple states.

Prosecutors argued that Nelson had demonstrated clear rational planning in the commission of his crimes. He had selected victims methodically. He had moved between cities to avoid detection. He had presented himself convincingly to his victims as a normal man. These were not the actions, the prosecution argued, of a man who did not know what he was doing.

The jury deliberated for one hour and ten minutes. They returned a verdict of guilty on both counts.

Nelson was sentenced to death.

At no point during the trial or in the weeks that followed did he provide a full confession. He maintained, in various statements, that he had not done the things he was convicted of. He also made statements suggesting he knew exactly what he had done. The contradiction was never resolved.

◆Part 9: The Execution

Earle Leonard Nelson was hanged at Vaughan Street Jail in Winnipeg on January 13, 1928. He was 30 years old.

On the morning of his execution he prayed with a Catholic priest. He had converted to Catholicism in prison. He read his Bible. He ate his last meal.

He walked to the gallows calmly. His final words were a prayer.

His body was buried in the grounds of the prison. No family members attended. No memorial was held.

The newspapers in the United States and Canada covered the execution extensively. The Gorilla Man, the Dark Strangler, Earle Nelson — the killer who had moved through boarding houses across two countries for 18 months — was gone.

The women he had killed had not been particularly prominent in life. They had been landladies, working women, women who opened their doors because that was their business and their livelihood. Their names appeared in the newspaper coverage primarily in lists attached to stories about their killer.

Twenty-two of them. Clara Newman in San Francisco in February 1926 to Lola Cowan in Winnipeg in June 1927. Eighteen months.

◆Part 10: The Institutional Failure

Earle Nelson was known to the American psychiatric system before he committed his first confirmed murder.

He had been committed to Napa State Hospital in 1918. He had escaped. He had been recaptured. He had been re-examined. He had been released.

The psychiatric assessments of the period were limited by the clinical vocabulary and the institutional priorities of the time. A man who had assaulted a child was assessed and committed. When he escaped and was recaptured, the assessment process began again. When he was released, the assessment had concluded that he was either safe to release or that there was no legal mechanism to hold him further.

Between his commitment in 1918 and his first murder in 1926, eight years passed. During those eight years he married, worked various jobs, came to the attention of police and psychiatric authorities on multiple occasions, and was never held long enough or securely enough to prevent what followed.

The United States in 1926 had no national registry of violent offenders, no mechanism for tracking the movement of individuals with documented histories of sexual violence across state lines, and no coordinated system for connecting murders in different jurisdictions to a single perpetrator.

Nelson crossed state lines after every murder. He was never connected to the crimes he left behind until after his arrest in Canada. By then 22 people were dead.

◆Part 11: The Victims

The 22 confirmed victims of Earle Nelson:

Clara Newman, 60, San Francisco, February 20, 1926. Laura Beale, 60, San Jose, March 2, 1926. Lillian St. Mary, 63, San Francisco, June 10, 1926. Ollie Russell, San Francisco, June 26, 1926. Mary Nesbit, 48, Oakland, August 16, 1926. Beata Withers, Portland, October 19, 1926. Virginia Grant, Portland, October 20, 1926. Mabel Fluke, Portland, October 21, 1926. unnamed victim, Seattle, November 1926. Blanche Myers, Portland, November 18, 1926. Florence Monks, 58, San Diego, December 18, 1926. Nettie Aluims, 22, Council Bluffs, December 23, 1926. Lola Belle Cowell, 8, Council Bluffs, December 23, 1926. Germania Harpin, Chicago, April 27, 1927. Mary McConnell, Chicago, April 27, 1927. Jennie Randolph, Philadelphia, May 27, 1927. Fannie May, Buffalo, June 1, 1927. Mary Sietsema, Detroit, June 3, 1927. Minnie May, Chicago, June 3, 1927. unnamed victim, Chicago, June 5, 1927. Emily Patterson, 40, Winnipeg, June 9, 1927. Lola Cowan, 14, Winnipeg, June 10, 1927.

Most were landlordies or women renting rooms. Most were middle-aged or older. Most had done nothing except open their door to a polite man with a Bible.

None of them are particularly well-remembered outside the context of the case. That is the last injustice the record does to them.

◆Part 12: The Record

The case of Earle Nelson is not primarily a story about a monster. It is a story about a broken system encountering a broken man and failing to hold either of them long enough to prevent the consequences.

Nelson was damaged before he was dangerous. The streetcar accident at age 10 left him with documented neurological changes that altered his behaviour and his capacity for normal social functioning. He was raised in a household defined by religious rigidity and emotional deprivation. He was institutionalised at 21 after his first known assault.

The institution released him. He escaped. He was recaptured. He was released again. This happened multiple times.

At no point did anyone with the authority and the tools to hold him permanently choose to do so. At no point did the American system of the 1920s — its courts, its hospitals, its police departments — develop a mechanism for tracking a man who crossed state lines after every crime he committed.

By the time Canadian police identified Earle Nelson as the boarding house killer, 22 people were dead across 12 cities in two countries.

The watch he tried to sell for 50 cents stopped him. Not the system. Not the psychiatric record. Not the pattern of crimes that connected across a continent.

A 50-cent watch in a small Manitoba town.

That is the whole story and it is not a comfortable one.
