Case File: 005 Corporate Crime

The Milk That Killed

◆Part 1: The Promise

The advertisements looked like medicine. White coats. Clinical language. Smiling healthy infants. Nestlé sales representatives dressed as nurses moved through hospitals in Nigeria, Ghana, the Philippines, Malaysia and dozens of other countries. They handed out free samples. They told new mothers their breast milk was insufficient. They said science had improved on nature.

They lied.

The product worked fine in Switzerland. In Zurich a mother could read the instructions. She owned a refrigerator. Clean water came from the tap. She could afford to keep buying tins when the free samples ran out.

In Lagos the calculation looked different entirely. Mothers who could not read the instructions mixed powder with contaminated river water. Mothers who could not afford regular tins stretched supplies thin. Babies received watered-down formula in dirty bottles with no way to sterilize them.

Babies starved. Babies caught infections. Babies died. Nestlé knew the difference. They sold the product anyway.

◆Part 2: The Business Model

To understand the scandal you need to understand the hook.

Breast milk works on supply and demand. The body produces what the baby consumes. Stop breastfeeding for several days, the supply drops. Stop long enough, it disappears entirely.

The free samples were not generosity. They were strategy.

A mother accepts the free tin. Uses it for two weeks. Her own milk supply begins to dry up. The free tin runs out. Now she faces a choice: buy the formula or watch her baby go hungry. Her body can no longer provide what it once could.

She is trapped. Nestlé created the trap deliberately.

In poor countries the trap closed completely. No regulation. No oversight. Officials sometimes received payments to look elsewhere. Hospitals received equipment donations in exchange for access to new mothers. The whole system pointed one direction. Toward the tin. Away from survival.

◆Part 3: The Reckoning

A British charity named War on Want published a report in 1974. The title said everything: The Baby Killer.

It documented what Nestlé and other formula companies did in developing nations. The aggressive marketing. The fake nurses. The free samples designed to end breastfeeding. The deaths that followed.

Nestlé responded by suing a Swiss organization that republished the report and called it "Nestlé Kills Babies."

The trial became a disaster for Nestlé. The Swiss judge found the activists technically guilty of defamation then told Nestlé directly that the moral responsibility for what happened rested with them.

Nestlé won the case. They lost the argument. The world saw the evidence and reached its own verdict.

The boycott started in 1977. American activists urged consumers to stop buying Nestlé products. Kit Kat. Nescafé. Milo. Everything. The campaign spread to Europe. University campuses. Churches. Unions. Nestlé called it communist agitation. The babies kept dying.

◆Part 4: The Code That Changed Nothing

In 1981 the World Health Organization passed the International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes. No free samples to mothers. No promotion in healthcare facilities. No financial incentives to health workers.

117 nations voted yes. One voted no. The United States under Ronald Reagan the only nation on earth that did.

The boycott suspended after Nestlé promised to follow the code voluntarily. Three years later activists found Nestlé violating the code in country after country. The promises meant nothing. The boycott restarted. It continues in some form to this day.

Forty years after the code passed, violations still get documented regularly. In 2018 an investigation found formula companies including Nestlé paying maternity hospital staff in China to recommend their products. In 2023 a major investigation found Nestlé selling formula with added sugar in poor countries while selling versions without sugar in wealthy ones.

Same company. Different standards depending on how much your government was watching.

◆Part 5: The Accountability Gap

Who answers for this?

Nestlé executives faced no criminal charges. No prison sentences. The company paid no meaningful fines relative to its size. It issued statements. Expressed concern. Pledged reform. Continued.

The pattern repeated decade after decade. Violation documented. Pledge issued. Violation continues. New pledge issued.

No international body holds corporations criminally responsible for marketing practices that kill children. The WHO code carried no enforcement mechanism.

Nestlé today ranks among the largest food companies on earth. Revenue around 90 billion dollars annually. It remains a boycott target for organizations on every inhabited continent.

The executives who made the decisions in the 1970s retired comfortably. The mothers who buried infants along the Niger River and the Ganges Delta got nothing. Not acknowledgment. Not compensation. Not the truth told plainly in any official setting.

Somewhere tonight a mother who cannot read the instructions mixes powder with water she does not know is contaminated. She is doing what the woman in the white coat suggested. She believes she is doing the right thing for her child. The company knows what comes next. They sold it anyway.
