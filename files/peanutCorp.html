Case File: 028 Corporate Crime

The Owner Said Turn Them Loose

Published 11 November 2026

How this file was sourced: official records only, listed in Sources below.

◆Part 1: The Release

The email read: okay, let's turn them loose then...Stewart.

It was sent on 21 August 2008. The federal indictment charged that the product had tested confirmed positive for salmonella. A retest came back negative. Stewart Parnell, owner and president of Peanut Corporation of America, told his plant manager Samuel Lightsey to release it.

In 2009 federal authorities identified the company's plant in Blakely, Georgia as the source of a nationwide salmonella outbreak.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention counted 714 people infected with the outbreak strain in 46 states. It recorded that infection may have contributed to 9 deaths.

On 21 September 2015 a federal judge sentenced Parnell to 336 months in prison. 28 years. The Justice Department called it the largest criminal sentence ever given in a food safety case.

◆Part 2: The Company

On or about 15 February 2001 a peanut plant in Blakely trading as Casey's Food Products changed its name to Peanut Corporation of America. Stewart Parnell of Lynchburg, Virginia became an owner and president.

The company roasted, blanched and chopped peanuts and made peanut butter and peanut paste. It sold them to food manufacturers, pet food manufacturers, food service distributors and retail outlets. It ran plants in Blakely, in Plainview, Texas and in Suffolk, Virginia. Its headquarters offices operated out of Parnell's home in Lynchburg.

In its last full fiscal year, October 2007 to September 2008, the 3 plants generated gross sales of about 30 million dollars.

The indictment described Parnell as the company's primary leader and decision maker. His brother Michael Parnell was vice president of P.P. Sales, a food broker that worked on behalf of the company. The Court of Appeals recorded that Michael managed the sale of peanut paste to the Kellogg Company.

A company brochure called it The Processor of the World's Finest Peanut Products.

Many customers required a Certificate of Analysis with every lot. The certificate stated that the lot had tested negative for bacteria.

◆Part 3: The Retests

The Court of Appeals summarised the trial evidence in 2018. At Stewart Parnell's direction, the company retested product that tested positive for salmonella until it obtained a negative result. It shipped product before receiving the test results. It shipped product after receiving confirmed positive results.

The indictment set out the emails. On 5 October 2006 a customer told the company that products it had received tested confirmed positive for salmonella. The grand jury charged that Parnell falsely replied: I am dumbfounded by what you have found. It is the first time in my over 26 years in the peanut business that I have ever seen any instance of this.

On 21 March 2007 he was told that salmonella results were not back and that part of a customer's order would be delayed. His reply opened with an expletive. Then: just ship it. I cannot afford to loose [sic] another customer.

On 5 April 2007 he was asked whether to short ship them again and wait for results or just ship. He answered in 1 word. SHIP.

In June 2008 Mary Wilkerson, the quality assurance director at Blakely, wrote to the manager of another company plant: I know you don't know this but we have a problem with the granulation line and salmonella at least every other week if not every week, but when retested by a different lab it comes back ok.

On 6 October 2008 Lightsey reported a positive for salmonella from the lab. Parnell replied: We need to discuss this, the time lapse. Besides the cost, it is costing us huge. 5 dollar signs followed. Then: and causing obviously a huge lapse in time from the time we pick up peanuts until the time we can invoice.

◆Part 4: The Future Lot Numbers

The largest scheme involved peanut paste for Kellogg's. The Court of Appeals recorded that from September 2007 the company assigned future lot numbers to samples it sent for testing. It used those results to write certificates for new lots. The lot being shipped had not been tested.

Between January 2008 and January 2009, more than 60% of the paste lots for Kellogg's did not undergo any microbiological testing.

The indictment gave an example. Lot 8028 was shipped on 26 January 2008 to the buyer it called Customer #1, which used the paste in peanut butter crackers. 4 samples from that 1 lot went to the lab under the numbers 8032, 8036, 8037 and 8038. 3 came back within specifications. They became the certificates for 3 later truckloads, shipped on 31 January, 4 February and 6 February 2008.

Sample 8036 tested confirmed positive for salmonella. The number was never used. It was skipped in production. Lot 8028 had already been shipped. The indictment charged that the customer was not told.

According to the indictment, each tanker held about 44,000 pounds of paste. The plant shipped 2 to 3 tanker loads a week to that customer. The grand jury charged that of about 118 lots shipped to it in 2008 and 2009, about 74, or 63%, were never tested.

The CDC recorded that Austin and Keebler peanut butter crackers were made by the Kellogg Company in North Carolina using peanut paste from Peanut Corporation of America. Kellogg put them on hold on 14 January 2009 and recalled them on 16 January.

◆Part 5: The Outbreak

On 10 November 2008 CDC PulseNet staff noted a small and highly dispersed cluster of 13 Salmonella Typhimurium isolates from 12 states. The DNA fingerprint had not been seen before in its database.

In Minnesota the outbreak strain was isolated from an open 5 pound container of King Nut creamy peanut butter. King Nut was made by Peanut Corporation of America in Blakely. It was not sold in grocery stores. It went to institutions, including hospitals, schools and care facilities.

Illnesses began between 1 September 2008 and 31 March 2009. The patients ranged from under 1 to 98 years old. Half were younger than 16. 24% of those with available information reported being hospitalized.

The CDC recorded that infection may have contributed to 9 deaths: 1 in Idaho, 3 in Minnesota, 1 in North Carolina, 2 in Ohio and 2 in Virginia.

On 28 January 2009 the company recalled all peanuts and peanut products processed at Blakely since 1 January 2007. The CDC recorded that more than 2,833 products made by other companies may have been made with the recalled ingredients.

At trial a CDC doctor testified to 714 known illnesses, 166 of which required hospitalization. The doctor estimated more than 20,000 illnesses across the United States.

◆Part 6: The Inspection

FDA investigators were at the Blakely plant on 14 days between 9 and 27 January 2009. Their Form 483 listed what they observed.

Observation 1 listed 12 results from the firm's own private laboratory testing that found salmonella in its products in 2007 and 2008. In each case the firm retested, received a negative and shipped the product in interstate commerce.

1 of them was Salmonella Typhimurium, found in peanut paste made on 26 September 2008. The investigators recorded that the paste line was not cleaned afterwards. The firm kept making paste on that line until the inspection began.

They recorded that the firm had not established that its roaster was adequate as a kill step for pathogenic bacteria. Raw peanuts were stored next to finished roasted peanuts. Finished product sat within 15 feet of a floor crack that swabbed positive for Salmonella Senftenberg. Mold was growing on the ceiling and walls of the finished product cooler. Rain water had leaked in around the skylights. On 9 January 2009 they saw a live roach and several dead roaches in the wash room.

On 16 January 2009 an FDA investigator asked Parnell, on speaker phone, whether he knew of any other positives in 2008. Investigator Janet Gray testified at trial that he said: this is not something that happens very often and I think I would remember something positive. He said he had no knowledge of any others.

She asked Wilkerson the same question. Gray testified that Wilkerson told her she was not working in QA at the beginning of the year and was not aware of any positives.

On 19 January 2009 Parnell emailed FDA officials asking to keep doing business. Representative Waxman later read out what he wrote: desperately at least need to turn the raw peanuts on our floor into money.

◆Part 7: The Hearing

On 11 February 2009 the House Energy and Commerce Subcommittee on Oversight and Investigations held a hearing on the outbreak.

Jeffrey Almer testified first. His mother Shirley was 72. She had twice been declared cancer free. She died on 21 December after eating peanut butter in a care facility. He told the subcommittee: Cancer couldn't claim her but peanut butter did.

Lou Tousignant testified that his father Clifford, a Korean War veteran awarded 3 Purple Hearts, ate contaminated peanut butter from the company in December 2008. He died on 12 January 2009. He was 78.

Peter Hurley, a police officer from Wilsonville, Oregon, testified on the same panel. His son Jacob, 3, was severely sickened after eating Austin crackers.

Chairman Bart Stupak then read Parnell an email he had sent his employees on 12 January 2009: We do not believe the salmonella came from our facility. As you probably know, we send hourly PB samples to an independent lab to test for salmonella during production of peanut butter and we have never found any salmonella at all.

Stupak told him the FDA had found salmonella in company products tested by private labs on 12 separate occasions between June 2007 and September 2008. He asked whether Parnell or any company official had ever placed food they knew to be contaminated into interstate commerce.

Parnell answered: Mr. Chairman and members of the committee, on the advice of my counsel, I respectfully decline to answer questions based on the protection afforded me under the United States Constitution.

Representative Walden asked whether either man would take the lid off a container of products made with their ingredients and eat them. Parnell gave the same answer. Asked whether he intended to refuse every question under the Fifth Amendment, he said: Yes. Lightsey said the same.

◆Part 8: The Verdict

On 20 February 2013 a 76 count indictment was unsealed in the Middle District of Georgia. It charged Stewart Parnell, Michael Parnell and Lightsey with conspiracy, mail and wire fraud, and introducing adulterated and misbranded food into interstate commerce with intent to defraud or mislead. Stewart Parnell, Lightsey and Wilkerson were also charged with obstruction of justice.

Daniel Kilgore, operations manager at Blakely from June 2002 to May 2008, pleaded guilty on the day the charges against him were filed. Lightsey also pleaded guilty. Both testified at the trial.

The trial ran 7 weeks. Prosecutors called 45 witnesses and put 1,001 documents into evidence. The government had agreed not to present evidence of deaths.

On 19 September 2014 the jury returned its verdicts. Stewart Parnell was convicted on 67 counts and acquitted of 1 count of wire fraud. Michael Parnell was convicted on 31 counts and acquitted of 12 counts of introducing adulterated food. Wilkerson was convicted of 1 count of obstruction.

The court found Stewart Parnell responsible for a loss of 144.5 million dollars and Michael Parnell for 45.6 million dollars.

On 21 September 2015 Stewart Parnell, 61, was sentenced to 336 months. Michael Parnell was sentenced to 240 months. Wilkerson was sentenced to 60 months. On 1 October 2015 Kilgore was sentenced to 72 months and Lightsey to 36 months.

The Justice Department stated that the court found the evidence presented at trial linked the company's contaminated peanut products to the victims' illnesses.

◆Part 9: The Appeals

The Parnells and Wilkerson appealed. They argued that jurors had been exposed to outside information that the outbreak caused 9 deaths. A juror swore in an affidavit that several jurors mentioned they had done their own research.

The trial court refused to credit that juror. It found she was biased in favour of Wilkerson, whom she had approached after the trial to express sympathy.

On 23 January 2018 the Court of Appeals assumed that several jurors had been exposed. It held that the exposure did not affect or contribute to the verdict. It called the evidence of guilt against both brothers overwhelming. It rejected the challenge to the loss figures and affirmed the judgment.

The brothers then claimed their trial lawyers had been ineffective for not seeking to move the trial out of the Albany Division. Their lawyers testified that the choice was deliberate. 1 of Stewart Parnell's attorneys explained the idea: get maybe one or two people who were in that [peanut] industry who thought, you know, this wasn't PCA's fault, this was FDA's fault.

On 11 August 2025 the Court of Appeals affirmed the denial of relief. It found the decision was a strategic one made by 2 teams of experienced counsel after a full investigation.

The lab found salmonella. The retest came back negative. The email said turn them loose.
