The Radium Girls: How America Poisoned Its Workers and Called It Progress

◆Part 1: The Dream Job

In 1917, being a radium girl was the best job a working class woman could get in America. The pay was excellent three times what you'd make at a factory. The work was clean, performed in well-lit studios. You sat at a table with other girls your age, painting numbers on watch dials.

And there was another perk. You glowed. The radium paint was luminescent. After a shift, the girls would look at themselves in the mirror and see their clothes, their hair, even their skin glowing faintly in the dark. They painted their nails with it. They painted their teeth to make their smiles glow at parties.

It was magical. It was glamorous. It was killing them.

The technique they were taught was simple. Dip the brush in radium paint, bring the brush to a fine point with your lips, paint the number, repeat. Lip, dip, paint. Hundreds of times per day.

The company assured them the paint was harmless. Radium was in medicines and health tonics. How could it be dangerous? The girls believed them. Why wouldn't they?

◆Part 2: The First Deaths

Grace Fryer started working at the United States Radium Corporation in 1917. She was 18 years old. By 1922, her teeth started falling out.

She went to doctor after doctor. None of them could diagnose the problem. One told her she had syphilis. The shame of that misdiagnosis kept her from seeking more medical help for months.

She wasn't the only one. Mollie Maggia had worked at the same facility. By 1923, her entire lower jaw had to be removed. The doctor lifted it out in pieces. The radium had destroyed the bone so completely that it just crumbled.

Mollie died on September 12, 1922. She was 24 years old. The death certificate listed the cause as ulcerative stomatitis. A mouth infection. It was easier than admitting they didn't know what had killed her.

But her family knew something was wrong. So did the company.

◆Part 3: What the Company Knew

The United States Radium Corporation knew radium was dangerous. They knew because their chemists and researchers worked with radium using lead screens, masks, and tongs. They never touched it with their bare hands. They certainly never put it in their mouths.

The scientists knew. The executives knew. The plant managers knew. And they said nothing to the dial painters.

In 1924, a company physicist named Edwin Leman conducted a study. His findings were clear: the studio was contaminated with dangerous levels of radiation. The practice of lip-pointing brushes meant the girls were ingesting radium with every brush they painted.

Leman wrote a report. He recommended immediate changes. Stop the lip-pointing technique.

The United States Radium Corporation buried the report. They didn't stop the lip-pointing technique. They didn't warn the girls. Instead, they told the girls the paint was harmless. When girls asked if it was safe, supervisors told them it was safe enough to eat.

◆Part 4: The Lawsuit

Grace Fryer wanted to sue. By 1927, she was in constant pain. Her spine was disintegrating. She was 28 years old and dying.

She went to lawyer after lawyer. All of them turned her down. Then she found Raymond Berry, a young attorney willing to take the case. He filed suit in 1927 on behalf of Grace Fryer and four other radium girls: Edna Hussman, Katherine Schaub, Quinta McDonald, and Albina Larice.

The company's response was to delay. They filed motion after motion. They challenged every piece of evidence. They argued that the statute of limitations had expired.

The legal battle dragged on for months. Then years. Meanwhile, the women were dying.

The company knew what they were doing. They were waiting for the plaintiffs to die. And the strategy almost worked.

◆Part 5: The Settlement and the Deaths

In June 1928, the company agreed to settle. Not because they admitted fault the settlement explicitly stated United States Radium Corporation did not admit any liability.

The terms were insulting. Each woman received $10,000 in cash. An annual pension of $600 for as long as they lived. For destroying their bodies. For lying to them. For knowing radium was dangerous and saying nothing.

The women were furious. But they were also dying. They needed the money. They accepted.

Grace Fryer died in 1933 at age 34. Quinta McDonald died in 1929 at age 24. Albina Larice died in 1933 at age 33. Katherine Schaub died in 1933 at age 35.

No executive was ever charged. No criminal prosecution was brought.

◆Part 6: What Changed and What Didn't

The radium girls' lawsuits didn't just win settlements. They changed American law. They established that workers could sue employers for occupational diseases. They established employer liability for failing to warn workers about known dangers. They became the foundation for modern occupational safety law.

The lip-pointing technique was banned. By the 1940s, the U.S. developed comprehensive radiation safety standards. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration was created in 1970, partly tracing its lineage to these cases.

None of this helped the radium girls. They were already dead. But it helped the workers who came after them. They paid for those protections with their lives.

The companies that killed these women are gone, dissolved into corporate history. The executives who made the decisions lived comfortable lives and died natural deaths, their wealth and freedom intact.

The women they killed died young, in pain, fighting for justice in courts designed to protect the companies that poisoned them.

That's the final count: dozens of confirmed dead, hundreds more likely dead, zero executives held criminally accountable. The system worked exactly as designed.
